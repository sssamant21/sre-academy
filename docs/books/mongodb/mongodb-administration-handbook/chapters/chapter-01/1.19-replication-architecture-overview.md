## 1. Replication Architecture and Purpose

MongoDB uses **replica sets** to maintain redundant copies of data across multiple `mongod` processes.

A basic architecture is:

```text
                   Application
                       │
                       ▼
                  MongoDB Driver
                       │
                       ▼
                  Replica Set
                       │
          ┌────────────┼────────────┐
          ▼            ▼            ▼
       Primary     Secondary A   Secondary B
          │            │            │
          └────── Replicated ───────┘
                   Dataset
```

Replication supports high availability, data redundancy, automatic primary election, member maintenance, recovery from member failure, read-routing options, and distributed copies of production data.

However:

```text
Replication ≠ Backup
```

Replication protects primarily against member or infrastructure failure. Logical mistakes can also replicate. Independent backup and recovery therefore remain mandatory.

---

## 2. Replica Set Architecture

A replica set is a group of `mongod` processes cooperating to maintain the same logical dataset. A normal production topology commonly contains one primary and one or more secondaries.

```text
Primary
   =
Replica-Set Role

Primary
   ≠
Permanent Machine Identity
```

The primary role can move between eligible members through an election.

---

## 3. Primary and Secondary Roles

### Primary

The primary normally handles application writes and records replication operations in its oplog. It can also serve reads according to application routing and read semantics. If it becomes unavailable, another eligible member can potentially become primary.

### Secondary

A secondary is normally a data-bearing member that maintains replicated state.

```text
Sync Source
     │
     ▼
Oplog Stream
     │
     ▼
Secondary
     │
     ├── Local Oplog
     │
     ▼
Apply Operations
     │
     ▼
Local Dataset
```

Secondaries can also serve reads when read preference permits. Because replication is asynchronous, secondary data can temporarily lag behind the primary.

---

## 4. Oplog Architecture

MongoDB replication is driven by the **operations log**, or **oplog**. Every replica-set member maintains its own oplog in the member-local `local` database as `local.oplog.rs`.

```text
Primary
  │
  ├── Application Data
  └── local.oplog.rs
           │
           ▼
      Replication Stream
           │
      ┌────┴────┐
      ▼         ▼
Secondary A  Secondary B
    │             │
    ├── Data      ├── Data
    └── Oplog     └── Oplog
```

The `local` database itself is member-local. The oplog is a core replication mechanism, not merely an audit log.

The oplog maintains a rolling history of replication operations. Its configured size should not be interpreted as an immutable hard physical ceiling; MongoDB can allow it to grow beyond its configured size under documented conditions. Detailed oplog sizing belongs in Chapter 10.

---

## 5. Sync Sources and Chained Replication

A secondary selects an eligible **sync source** and does not necessarily replicate directly from the primary.

```text
             Primary
                │
          ┌─────┴─────┐
          ▼           ▼
   Secondary A    Secondary B
          │
          ▼
   Secondary C
```

MongoDB can change synchronization sources as topology and operating conditions change. This behavior is often described as **chained replication** when members synchronize through other secondaries. Detailed sync-source selection belongs in Chapter 10.

---

## 6. Asynchronous Replication and Lag

MongoDB replication is asynchronous.

```text
Primary Progress
       │
       │   Replication Delay
       ▼
Secondary Progress
```

A secondary being slightly behind the primary is not automatically unhealthy. Distinguish short replication delay from persistent replication lag.

Sustained lag can result from high write rate, slow secondary storage, CPU saturation, network latency or loss, infrastructure throttling, maintenance activity, or insufficient member capacity. The constrained component may be the secondary rather than the primary.

---

## 7. Oplog Window and Recovery Margin

Replication lag must be interpreted relative to available oplog history.

> **Replication lag without oplog-window context is incomplete evidence.**

```text
Secondary Requires Catch-Up
          │
          ▼
Are Required Oplog Entries Available?
        ┌─┴─┐
        │   │
       Yes  No
        │   │
        ▼   ▼
     Normal  Member Stale
     Catch-Up    │
                 ▼
             Resync /
           Initial Sync
```

A useful operational concept is:

```text
Recovery Margin
      =
Available Oplog History
      -
Required Catch-Up History
```

Exact monitoring and alert design belongs in Chapter 10.

---

## 8. Stale Members and Resynchronization

A secondary can fall far enough behind that the oplog entries it requires are no longer available from an appropriate sync source.

```text
Secondary Falls Behind
        │
        ▼
Required Oplog Entries
No Longer Available
        │
        ▼
Member Becomes Stale
        │
        ▼
Resynchronization Required
```

A stale member cannot simply continue ordinary replication using unavailable oplog history. Oplog capacity is therefore a **recoverability design decision**, not merely a storage setting.

---

## 9. Write Acknowledgement and MongoDB 8.x Majority Semantics

Replication participates in client write acknowledgement according to the effective write concern.

```text
Write Executed
      ≠
Write Acknowledged
      ≠
Applied Everywhere
```

### MongoDB 8.x Majority Acknowledgement

At a high level, `{ w: "majority" }` acknowledgement can occur after the required majority of data-bearing voting members have durably written the relevant oplog entry. Application of that operation to a secondary's user collection can continue asynchronously.

```text
Majority Acknowledged
        ≠
Applied to User Collection
on Every Acknowledging Secondary
```

Detailed read concern, write concern, sessions, and causal consistency belong in Chapter 4.

---

## 10. Elections, Voting Majority, and Fault Tolerance

When the primary becomes unavailable, eligible members can participate in an election.

```text
Primary Unavailable
        │
        ▼
Voting Members
        │
        ▼
Can Required Majority Communicate?
        │
     ┌──┴──┐
     ▼     ▼
    Yes    No
     │     │
     ▼     ▼
 Election  No New Primary
     │
     ▼
New Primary
```

Primary availability depends on voting topology and communication between voting members.

```text
Processes Running ≠ Primary Availability
More Members ≠ Automatically More Election Fault Tolerance
```

MongoDB replica sets can contain more members than voters, but only up to seven members can vote. Detailed election mechanics belong in Chapter 9.

---

## 11. Priority and Specialized Members

Priority influences a member's likelihood of becoming primary. A `priority: 0` member cannot become primary.

A hidden secondary is data-bearing, has priority 0, cannot become primary, is not normally client-discoverable, and may still vote when configured as a voting member.

A delayed member intentionally maintains data at an earlier point in replication history. Its configured delay must fit within the available oplog history.

```text
Configured Delay
       <
Available Oplog Window
```

Detailed configuration belongs in Chapter 9.

---

## 12. Arbiters and PSA Architecture

An arbiter participates in elections but does not maintain application data and cannot become primary.

```text
Arbiter ≠ Data-Bearing Secondary
Arbiter ≠ Data Redundancy
```

A primary-secondary-arbiter topology has different data-redundancy and majority-write availability characteristics from a three-data-bearing-member replica set. Multiple arbiters should not be treated as a normal strategy for increasing high availability. Detailed design belongs in Chapter 9.

---

## 13. Initial Synchronization

A new or resynchronized data-bearing member must establish a usable copy of the dataset before normal secondary replication can continue.

```text
New / Resynchronized Member
          │
          ▼
Select Sync Source
          │
          ▼
Establish Dataset
          │
          ▼
Catch Up Replication State
          │
          ▼
Normal Secondary Operation
```

> **Initial synchronization is a capacity event, not a free background operation.**

It can consume substantial network bandwidth, storage I/O, CPU, and sync-source resources. Detailed initial-sync operations belong in Chapter 10.

---

## 14. Rollback Architecture

Replica-set failover can create situations where a former primary contains writes that are not part of the replica set's surviving authoritative history.

```text
Former Primary
      │
      ▼
Divergent History
      │
      ▼
Rejoins Replica Set
      │
      ▼
History Reconciliation
      │
      ▼
Rollback if Required
```

Rollback reconciles divergent replica-set history.

```text
Rollback ≠ Backup Restore
```

Detailed rollback mechanics and recovery procedures belong in Chapter 10.

---

## 15. Flow Control

Replication performance can influence primary write performance. MongoDB can use flow control to manage primary write throughput when majority-commit replication lag creates pressure.

```text
Write Workload
      │
      ▼
Replication Lag Pressure
      │
      ▼
Flow Control
      │
      ▼
Primary Write
Throughput / Latency
```

```text
Flow-Control Wait ≠ Lock Wait
```

The root cause can exist on a secondary while the visible application symptom appears on the primary.

---

## 16. Failure-Domain and Network Architecture

Replica-set architecture should consider host, rack, availability-zone, network, storage, and regional failure domains.

> **Topology design must consider both data placement and voting placement.**

Replica-set members require broad member-to-member connectivity for heartbeats, elections, sync-source selection, replication, and topology changes. Network architecture is therefore part of MongoDB availability architecture.

---

## 17. Application Failover and Recovery

Database failover and application recovery are not the same event.

```text
Primary Failure
      │
      ▼
Failure Detection
      │
      ▼
Election
      │
      ▼
New Primary
      │
      ▼
Driver Topology Discovery
      │
      ▼
Server Selection / Retry
      │
      ▼
Application Recovery
```

```text
MongoDB Election Time ≠ Total Application Recovery Time
```

Application recovery can depend on election duration, driver topology discovery, server selection, connection state, retryable operations, timeout configuration, and application retry logic.

---

## 18. Production Failure Patterns

The principal production patterns are:

1. **Secondary Replication Lag** — a member cannot sustain the replication rate.
2. **Shrinking Oplog Recovery Margin** — lag approaches available replication history.
3. **Stale Secondary / Resync Required** — required oplog entries are unavailable.
4. **Primary Failure and Election** — database failover plus driver/application recovery.
5. **Repeated Elections** — instability requiring root-cause analysis.
6. **Voting-Majority Loss / Network Partition** — processes may run while no electable majority exists.
7. **Slow Secondary Storage or CPU** — downstream resource constraints create lag.
8. **Initial-Sync Capacity Pressure** — synchronization consumes production resources.
9. **Replication / Flow-Control Pressure** — lag affects primary write throughput.
10. **Stale Secondary Read** — asynchronous replication affects observed data.
11. **PSA / Arbiter Availability Limitation** — voting topology differs from data redundancy.
12. **Replication Mistaken for Backup** — logical mistakes propagate to replicated members.

## 19. Replication Troubleshooting

Use an evidence-driven model:

```text
Replication Problem
       │
       ▼
Primary / Election State
       │
       ├── Primary Present?
       ├── Voting Majority?
       └── Members Reachable?
       │
       ▼
Replication Progress
       │
       ├── Lag?
       ├── Required Oplog Entries Available?
       ├── Member Stale?
       └── Initial Sync?
       │
       ▼
Sync Architecture
       │
       ├── Current Sync Source?
       ├── Sync-Source Changes?
       └── Chained Replication?
       │
       ▼
Resource Evidence
       │
       ├── CPU
       ├── Storage
       └── Network
       │
       ▼
Replication Controls
       │
       ├── Flow Control
       ├── Write Concern
       └── Member Configuration
       │
       ▼
Root Cause
```

Do not begin by changing replica-set configuration. First identify the constrained or failed layer.

---

## 20. Evidence Collection

Collect evidence across six domains:

1. **Topology and Election** — current primary, member states, voting configuration, priorities, hidden/delayed status, arbiters, election events.
2. **Replication** — lag, oplog window, required catch-up history, stale state, sync source, sync-source changes, initial sync, rollback evidence.
3. **Write Path** — write workload, effective write concern, acknowledgement latency, flow control.
4. **Member Resources** — CPU, memory, storage latency, IOPS, throughput, queue depth, infrastructure throttling.
5. **Network** — member-to-member connectivity, latency, packet loss, DNS, connection failures, zone/region events.
6. **Application** — topology-change events, server-selection errors, retries, read preference, effective write concern, application-observed recovery time.

Always correlate evidence by timestamp.

---

## 21. Monitoring and Alerting

Production replication monitoring should include primary availability, voting majority, member health, election activity, replication lag, oplog window, recovery margin, sync source, initial sync, flow control, network health, and member resources.

Important alert categories include no primary, unavailable members, repeated elections, sustained replication lag, shrinking oplog recovery margin, stale-member risk, initial-sync failure, sustained flow-control pressure, storage pressure, and member-to-member network instability.

Avoid universal thresholds. Thresholds should reflect workload characteristics, normal replication baseline, expected maintenance duration, oplog window, RPO, RTO, and failure-domain design.

---

## 22. Production Anti-Patterns

Avoid:

- treating replication as backup;
- assuming secondaries are always current;
- requiring zero replication lag at every instant;
- monitoring lag without oplog-window context;
- assuming every secondary always replicates directly from the primary;
- treating configured oplog size as an absolute hard ceiling;
- assuming a stale member can always catch up normally;
- assuming more members automatically provide more election fault tolerance;
- equating running process count with primary availability;
- permanently associating the primary role with one server;
- assuming `priority: 0` members can become primary;
- assuming hidden members cannot vote;
- configuring delayed members without considering oplog history;
- treating arbiters as data-bearing members or data redundancy;
- treating PSA as equivalent to three data-bearing members;
- using multiple arbiters as a routine HA strategy;
- ignoring member-to-member network requirements;
- diagnosing all replication problems from the primary;
- ignoring secondary storage and CPU constraints;
- confusing flow-control waits with lock waits;
- assuming majority acknowledgement means applied everywhere;
- treating every election as an incident;
- accepting repeated elections without investigation;
- treating initial sync as cost-free background work;
- changing election settings before root-cause analysis; and
- scaling the primary while ignoring constrained secondaries.

## 24. Key DBA Takeaways

1. MongoDB production replication is based on replica sets.
2. The primary role is dynamic rather than permanently attached to a server.
3. Secondaries replicate asynchronously.
4. Every replica-set member maintains its own oplog.
5. A secondary does not necessarily synchronize directly from the primary.
6. Replication lag must be evaluated with oplog-window context.
7. Oplog capacity is a recoverability design decision.
8. Stale members require resynchronization rather than ordinary catch-up.
9. Write execution, acknowledgement, and application everywhere are different milestones.
10. MongoDB 8.x majority acknowledgement does not necessarily mean every acknowledging secondary has already applied the user-document change.
11. Primary availability depends on voting topology and communication.
12. Running process count does not determine election availability.
13. More members do not automatically provide more fault tolerance.
14. `priority: 0` members cannot become primary.
15. Hidden members can still vote when configured to vote.
16. Delayed members depend on sufficient oplog history.
17. Arbiters vote but do not store application data or increase data redundancy.
18. PSA is not equivalent to three data-bearing members.
19. Initial synchronization can materially affect production capacity.
20. Rollback is replication-history reconciliation, not backup restoration.
21. Secondary replication capacity can influence primary write latency through flow control.
22. Topology must account for both data placement and voting placement.
23. Member-to-member network connectivity is part of database availability.
24. Election time is only one component of total application recovery.
25. Replication does not replace independent backup and recovery.
