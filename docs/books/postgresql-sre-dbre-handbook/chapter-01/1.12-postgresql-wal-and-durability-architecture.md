# 1.12 — PostgreSQL WAL and Durability Architecture

**Status:** Draft → Technical Review Pending  
**Validation baseline:** PostgreSQL 18.x official documentation

## Purpose

Write-Ahead Logging (WAL) is central to PostgreSQL crash recovery, durability, physical replication, continuous archiving, point-in-time recovery, and logical decoding. SRE/DBRE teams must understand both the WAL write path and the operational consequences of WAL retention, flush latency, configuration changes, and archive/replication failures.

## Write-Ahead Principle

```text
Modify data
  -> generate WAL record
  -> WAL enters WAL buffers
  -> required WAL is written/flushed to durable storage
  -> commit can be acknowledged according to durability settings
  -> dirty data pages may be written later
```

The essential ordering is that the WAL describing a change reaches the required durability point before the corresponding changed data page is relied upon as durable.

## WAL Levels

PostgreSQL 18 supports `minimal`, `replica`, and `logical` WAL levels. The default is `replica`. `logical` includes additional information required for logical decoding. `minimal` is incompatible with the WAL information requirements of continuous archiving and streaming replication.

Treat `wal_level` as an architectural setting, not a casual tuning parameter.

## WAL Buffers and Flush

Backends insert WAL records into WAL buffers. WAL is written and flushed through PostgreSQL's WAL mechanisms. Commit latency can therefore be sensitive to storage fsync latency and concurrent WAL pressure.

`wal_buffers` can matter on systems with high WAL output, but tuning must be evidence-based.

## Commit Durability

Settings such as `fsync`, `synchronous_commit`, and `full_page_writes` influence durability/performance behavior. Disabling durability protections can create data-loss or corruption exposure after OS or hardware failure and must never be presented as a routine performance fix.

## Full-Page Writes

With `full_page_writes` enabled, PostgreSQL logs a full page image on the first modification after a checkpoint when required to protect against partial-page writes. More frequent checkpoints can therefore increase WAL volume as well as data-file write pressure.

## Checkpoint Relationship

A checkpoint establishes a recovery boundary where data files are guaranteed to contain changes preceding the checkpoint's redo requirement. Crash recovery can begin from the appropriate redo location rather than replaying the entire historical WAL stream.

Checkpoint frequency is controlled primarily by time and WAL-volume conditions, including `checkpoint_timeout` and `max_wal_size`.

## WAL Segments and Recycling

WAL is stored in segment files under `pg_wal`. Segments can be recycled or removed when PostgreSQL no longer needs them for crash recovery and when replication, archiving, slots, backups, or other retention requirements do not require them.

Never manually delete WAL files to solve disk pressure.

## WAL Archiving

Continuous archiving copies completed WAL segments to an archive destination using the configured archive mechanism. Archive failure can cause WAL accumulation on the primary. Monitor both archive success/failure and the age of the last successful archive.

## Replication and Slots

Physical and logical replication depend on WAL. Replication slots can intentionally retain required WAL for consumers. A failed or abandoned consumer can therefore turn a replication problem into a primary-storage incident.

## WAL Monitoring

Monitor:

- WAL bytes generated over time
- WAL generation rate
- `pg_wal` filesystem usage
- `pg_stat_wal`
- archiver success/failure
- replication lag
- replication-slot retained WAL
- WAL sender/receiver health
- checkpoint frequency and write/sync time
- storage flush latency

## Alerting

Use rate- and capacity-aware alerts. High WAL generation by itself can be legitimate during bulk loads, index operations, vacuum-related activity, or application bursts. Alert more aggressively when high generation combines with failed archiving, inactive replication slots, increasing replication lag, or projected `pg_wal` exhaustion.

## Failure Scenarios

Important WAL failure patterns include archive-command failure, replication-slot retention, standby outage, WAL-device latency, excessive checkpoint frequency, sudden bulk-write amplification, and unsafe durability settings.

## Troubleshooting Workflow

1. Measure current WAL generation and `pg_wal` capacity.
2. Inspect `pg_stat_wal` and checkpoint statistics.
3. Inspect replication slots and replication state.
4. Verify archive success and archive destination health.
5. Correlate WAL growth with workload changes and maintenance operations.
6. Check storage write/fsync latency.
7. Review recent configuration changes.
8. Preserve recovery requirements before removing any retention mechanism.

## Production Case Study — Inactive Slot

A logical consumer stops for several hours. Its replication slot remains active as a retention requirement, causing the primary to retain WAL. Disk utilization rises rapidly. The safe response is to identify the slot and consumer, determine whether the consumer can resume, estimate retained WAL and remaining headroom, and only drop/recreate the slot when the recovery/data-loss consequences are explicitly understood.

## SRE/DBRE Principle

> WAL is simultaneously a durability mechanism and a capacity dependency. Monitor the WAL pipeline end to end: generation, flush, archive, replication consumption, retention, and available storage.
