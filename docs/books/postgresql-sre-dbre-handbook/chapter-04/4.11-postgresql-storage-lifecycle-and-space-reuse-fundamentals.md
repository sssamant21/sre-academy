## 4.11.1 Purpose

PostgreSQL storage does not follow a simple model in which deleting data immediately reduces filesystem usage. Production SREs and DBREs must distinguish logically live data, obsolete tuple versions, dead tuples, internally reusable space, physically allocated relation storage, filesystem or PVC free capacity, and space physically returned to the operating system.

The production objective is predictable storage behavior, sustainable reuse, adequate headroom, and safe reclamation rather than keeping every relation at its theoretical minimum size.

## 4.11.2 Storage Lifecycle

```text
INSERT / UPDATE / DELETE
          |
          v
       Tuples
          |
          v
         MVCC
          |
     +----+----+
     |         |
    LIVE    OBSOLETE
               |
               v
            VACUUM
               |
               v
        REUSABLE SPACE
               |
       +-------+-------+
       |               |
       v               v
 Future reuse     Physical reclamation
```

Logical data modification does not necessarily cause equivalent immediate physical-storage reduction.

## 4.11.3 Logical Data and Physical Storage

`DELETE` removes qualifying rows logically, but the corresponding physical storage is not necessarily returned immediately to the filesystem. Old tuple versions remain subject to MVCC visibility rules until they can become removable and VACUUM can process them.

`UPDATE` similarly creates tuple-version churn. High-update tables can therefore generate substantial physical churn even when logical row counts remain stable.

## 4.11.4 HOT Updates

Heap-Only Tuple updates can reduce some index-maintenance overhead when PostgreSQL can satisfy HOT requirements, but HOT does not eliminate the MVCC tuple lifecycle. Detailed HOT mechanics belong with later MVCC, vacuum, and index discussions.

## 4.11.5 Fundamental Storage States

Production analysis should distinguish live application-visible data, obsolete tuple versions, reusable relation space, physically allocated relation storage, and capacity returned to the operating system. Allocated bytes are not equivalent to active application data.

## 4.11.6 Internal Reuse vs. Filesystem Reclamation

Reusable PostgreSQL space is not the same as free filesystem space. A large relation may contain substantial reusable internal space while continuing to occupy the same approximate physical file size.

Standard VACUUM primarily supports reuse inside the relation. Physical reclamation requires removable trailing pages or a controlled rewrite/compaction operation.

## 4.11.7 Standard VACUUM and Tail Truncation

Routine VACUUM processes obsolete tuple versions and makes their space available for future reuse. It does not generally shrink a relation simply because reusable space exists in the middle.

VACUUM can attempt to return empty trailing pages by truncating the end of a table when safe. Relation truncation has stronger locking implications and must be considered in production latency and concurrency planning.

## 4.11.8 Free Space Map

The Free Space Map tracks approximate available page space and helps PostgreSQL locate pages that can accommodate new tuples instead of always extending a relation. It is a page-space navigation mechanism, not exact filesystem-capacity accounting.

The Visibility Map serves a different purpose: visibility metadata must not be interpreted as free-space information.

## 4.11.9 Internal Free Space Is Not Automatically Bloat

Allocated relation size, live tuple size, dead tuple space, reusable free space, and filesystem free space are different quantities. A large relation after a purge does not automatically prove that VACUUM failed or that all remaining allocation is bloat.

Detailed bloat analysis follows in Section 4.14.

## 4.11.10 Steady-State Reuse

For high-churn OLTP systems, a stable physical footprint with healthy reusable space can be preferable to repeatedly shrinking and regrowing relations. Before physically reclaiming storage, determine whether the workload is likely to need that capacity again.

## 4.11.11 VACUUM FULL

`VACUUM FULL` is a controlled table rewrite, not routine storage maintenance. It can reclaim substantial filesystem space but requires an `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE` lock and additional working capacity while the rewritten relation exists.

Never blindly run `VACUUM FULL` because a filesystem is nearly full. Stabilize capacity first, then evaluate reclamation under change control.

A production rewrite preflight must include relation, index and TOAST size, free working capacity, locking impact, WAL generation, replica impact, storage IOPS and throughput, backup overlap, HA readiness, maintenance window, monitoring, abort criteria, and recovery plan.

## 4.11.12 TRUNCATE and Large Purges

When the requirement is to remove all table contents, evaluate whether `TRUNCATE` matches the intended semantics rather than deleting every row. `TRUNCATE` still requires review for locking, foreign keys, triggers, transactional behavior, application expectations, and recovery.

Large DELETE operations are production maintenance events. They can create substantial WAL, I/O, dead tuples, replication traffic, replica lag, and vacuum work. Large purges should have capacity, WAL, I/O, replication, backup, SLO, rollback, and abort criteria reviewed before execution.

Controlled batch deletion can reduce transactional blast radius and improve throttling and observation, but it does not eliminate the total WAL, vacuum, or storage work.

## 4.11.13 Long-Running Transactions

Storage reuse depends not only on VACUUM running but also on whether obsolete tuple versions are removable. Long-running transactions can preserve older visibility horizons and delay cleanup. `idle in transaction` sessions therefore require operational visibility, but termination must follow application-impact and rollback analysis rather than generic automation.

## 4.11.14 VACUUM as a Capacity Mechanism

Autovacuum and manual VACUUM are part of storage-capacity engineering. The correct production question is not merely whether autovacuum is enabled or running, but whether vacuum throughput is keeping pace with workload churn.

Warning signals include rising dead-tuple estimates, unexpected relation growth, repeated vacuum cancellation, long-running transactions, heavy UPDATE/DELETE churn, worsening latency, and shrinking storage runway.

Detailed vacuum engineering follows in Section 4.13.

## 4.11.15 Heap, Index, and TOAST Storage

A logical table can consume storage through its heap, indexes, TOAST structures, and supporting forks. Diagnose these components separately before choosing remediation. A large total relation size alone does not identify whether the heap, an index, or TOAST is the dominant growth source.

## 4.11.16 Relation-Size Diagnostics

```sql
SELECT
    pg_size_pretty(pg_relation_size('public.orders')) AS heap_size,
    pg_size_pretty(pg_indexes_size('public.orders')) AS indexes_size,
    pg_size_pretty(pg_total_relation_size('public.orders')) AS total_size;
```

Correlate size with activity:

```sql
SELECT
    schemaname,
    relname,
    n_live_tup,
    n_dead_tup,
    n_tup_ins,
    n_tup_upd,
    n_tup_del,
    last_vacuum,
    last_autovacuum,
    vacuum_count,
    autovacuum_count
FROM pg_stat_user_tables
ORDER BY n_dead_tup DESC;
```

Tuple statistics are operational estimates rather than exact physical byte accounting.

## 4.11.17 Growth and Capacity Runway

A single relation-size measurement is insufficient. Monitor current size, growth rate, growth acceleration, DML churn, vacuum behavior, infrastructure free capacity, and storage performance.

A useful infrastructure approximation is:

```text
Approximate runway = available storage capacity / positive net storage growth rate
```

Use actual filesystem, PVC, or provider-storage occupancy growth for infrastructure runway calculations.

## 4.11.18 Capacity and Performance

Storage capacity and storage performance are separate reliability dimensions. Monitor used percentage, free GB, growth rate and runway alongside read/write latency, IOPS, throughput, queue depth, database latency, and application SLOs. A purge or rewrite can cause an I/O incident before storage reaches exhaustion.

## 4.11.19 Replication, HA, and Backup

Large DELETEs and rewrites can generate substantial WAL and replica replay work. High-impact storage maintenance requires replication monitoring, standby-capacity validation, failover readiness, and a recovery procedure.

Avoid overlapping major reclamation work with heavy backups unless the combined I/O, WAL, network, and capacity profile has been tested and reviewed.

## 4.11.20 Kubernetes and Managed PostgreSQL

For PostgreSQL on Kubernetes, correlate relation growth with filesystem use, PVC capacity, StorageClass behavior, volume IOPS/throughput, expansion capability, topology, operator reconciliation, failover, and backup. PostgreSQL reusable space is not equivalent to Kubernetes PVC free capacity.

For managed PostgreSQL services, correlate PostgreSQL object telemetry with provider-supported storage telemetry and use provider-supported remediation. Do not copy self-managed filesystem procedures into managed-service operations without validation.

## 4.11.21 Emergency Low-Space Runbook

1. Identify the affected cluster, database, and storage volume.
2. Measure free capacity, net growth, and approximate runway.
3. Identify fastest-growing databases and relations.
4. Separate heap, index, and TOAST consumption.
5. Review INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE, batch, ETL, purge, backup, and maintenance activity.
6. Check dead-tuple trends, long transactions, `idle in transaction`, and vacuum activity.
7. Check WAL and temporary-storage growth separately.
8. Check replication health and relevant retention mechanisms.
9. Protect remaining capacity and pause optional growth when approved.
10. Expand storage where safely supported.
11. Resolve transaction or vacuum blockers safely.
12. Determine whether internally reusable space will be reused.
13. Evaluate physical reclamation only after stabilization.
14. Monitor database and application SLOs until growth stabilizes.
15. Update forecasts and complete RCA/prevention actions.

## 4.11.22 Unsafe Emergency Actions

Never manually delete PostgreSQL relation, FSM, VM, or TOAST files as a storage-reclamation technique. Do not blindly run `VACUUM FULL`, terminate every session, disable autovacuum, drop indexes without dependency analysis, or restart PostgreSQL merely hoping capacity will appear.

## 4.11.23 Automation Boundaries

Automate observation aggressively: relation-size collection, heap/index/TOAST breakdown, growth calculations, dead-tuple trends, vacuum history, long-transaction detection, filesystem/PVC capacity, I/O telemetry, replication correlation, top-growing-object reports, and runway forecasting.

Require controlled approval for terminating transactions, large DELETEs, TRUNCATE, `VACUUM FULL`, CLUSTER, REINDEX, destructive object changes, storage changes, and major autovacuum tuning. Never generic-automate destructive remediation based solely on a capacity threshold.

## 4.11.24 Production Case Studies

### Historical purge

A 3 TB table has 1.5 TB of historical rows deleted. Filesystem usage remains high after standard VACUUM. Do not conclude that VACUUM failed. Determine whether obsolete tuples were processed, how much space is internally reusable, whether the relation will regrow, and whether OS-level reclamation is actually required.

### Autovacuum falling behind

If dead tuples, table size, and query latency increase while autovacuum is frequently active, investigate churn, vacuum throughput, worker availability, I/O, long transactions, lock conflicts, indexes, and application concurrency. Autovacuum presence does not prove autovacuum effectiveness.

### Unsafe emergency rewrite

If a filesystem is 94% utilized and the largest relation is 800 GB, do not immediately run `VACUUM FULL`. Calculate runway, stop optional growth, expand capacity where possible, identify actual storage composition, stabilize production, and only then plan reclamation under change control.

## 4.11.25 Canonical Production Rules

1. Logical deletion does not imply immediate physical storage release.
2. UPDATE can create obsolete tuple versions and storage churn.
3. MVCC determines when old tuple versions can become removable.
4. VACUUM enables obsolete tuple space to become reusable.
5. Internal reuse and filesystem reclamation are different.
6. Standard VACUUM primarily supports internal reuse.
7. VACUUM can attempt to truncate empty trailing pages.
8. Free pages in the middle do not simply shorten a relation file.
9. FSM tracks reusable page space but is not exact filesystem accounting.
10. Internal reusable space is not automatically bloat.
11. Stable reusable storage can be healthy.
12. Evaluate regrowth before physically reclaiming reusable space.
13. `VACUUM FULL` is a rewrite operation, not routine maintenance.
14. Rewrite operations require locking and capacity analysis.
15. Stabilize capacity before aggressive reclamation.
16. Large DELETE operations require production planning.
17. Long-running transactions can delay cleanup.
18. Autovacuum effectiveness is part of capacity management.
19. Heap, indexes, and TOAST must be analyzed separately.
20. PostgreSQL object metrics and infrastructure metrics must be correlated.
21. Monitor growth trajectory and capacity runway, not only current size.
22. Storage capacity and I/O performance are separate reliability dimensions.
23. Large maintenance operations require replication, HA, and backup analysis.
24. Kubernetes and managed-service storage boundaries must be respected.
25. Never manually delete PostgreSQL-managed relation files.
26. Automate monitoring and forecasting aggressively; automate destructive remediation conservatively.
27. Significant storage incidents require RCA and prevention actions.
