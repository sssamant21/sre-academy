# 4.11 -- PostgreSQL Storage Lifecycle and Space Reuse Fundamentals

**Status:** Draft

**Workflow:** Draft -> Technical Review -> Production Review -> Revised Final / Canonical Edition

**Chapter:** Chapter 4 -- PostgreSQL Storage Architecture and Capacity Management

PostgreSQL storage does not follow a simple allocate-then-immediately-free lifecycle. Its MVCC architecture, heap pages, free space map, VACUUM processing, relation truncation, table rewrites, and operating-system filesystems create several distinct meanings of "free space." Understanding those meanings is essential for capacity planning and for avoiding incorrect emergency actions.

## 4.11.1 Why Space Reuse Matters

An `UPDATE` or `DELETE` does not normally remove an old row version immediately. PostgreSQL must retain row versions while they can still be visible to transactions. Once an old version is no longer needed, VACUUM can reclaim its space for reuse by future rows.

This produces an important lifecycle:

```text
INSERT / UPDATE / DELETE
          |
          v
    Relation pages
          |
          v
 Old tuple versions
          |
          v
   Become removable
          |
          v
       VACUUM
          |
          v
 Internal free space
          |
          +----------------------+
          |                      |
          v                      v
 Reused by future rows     End pages may be
                           truncated when safe
                                  |
                                  v
                           Space may return
                              to the OS
```

The key operational lesson is that space becoming reusable inside PostgreSQL is not the same as filesystem space being returned to the operating system.

## 4.11.2 Four Storage States

For capacity engineering, distinguish at least four states:

1. **Live allocated space** -- pages containing current tuples and relation structures.
2. **Dead or obsolete tuple space** -- storage occupied by row versions that are no longer useful but have not yet been reclaimed.
3. **Reusable internal free space** -- space PostgreSQL can reuse inside an existing relation after cleanup.
4. **Filesystem free space** -- blocks no longer allocated to the PostgreSQL relation and available to the operating system or storage service.

These states must not be treated as interchangeable.

## 4.11.3 Internal Reuse vs. Operating-System Reclamation

Standard `VACUUM` removes dead row versions and makes their space available for future reuse. It generally does not shrink the relation file merely because reusable space exists in the middle of the file.

Therefore:

```text
Table size on filesystem
        !=
Live row data size
```

and:

```text
VACUUM completed
        !=
Filesystem usage must decrease
```

This distinction is foundational for Sections 4.12 through 4.17.

## 4.11.4 Free Space Map

PostgreSQL maintains a Free Space Map (FSM) for heap and most index relations. The FSM records available space in relation pages so PostgreSQL can locate pages with room for new tuples rather than always extending the relation.

Conceptually:

```text
Existing relation
   |
   +-- Page 1: mostly full
   +-- Page 2: free space
   +-- Page 3: full
   +-- Page 4: free space
             |
             v
            FSM
             |
             v
New tuple can reuse an existing page
instead of extending the relation
```

The FSM was introduced in detail in Section 4.6; here its importance is space lifecycle and reuse.

## 4.11.5 Visibility and Reuse Are Different Concepts

The Visibility Map and Free Space Map serve different purposes.

- The FSM tracks available page space.
- The Visibility Map tracks page-level visibility information used by VACUUM and index-only scans.

Do not interpret an all-visible page as an empty or reusable page. Visibility status and free-space availability are different dimensions.

## 4.11.6 Relation Growth

A relation grows when PostgreSQL requires additional pages and cannot satisfy the allocation from appropriate existing free space.

Simplified model:

```text
New tuple
   |
   v
Reusable page available?
   |             |
  YES            NO
   |             |
   v             v
Reuse page    Extend relation
                  |
                  v
             Filesystem growth
```

Sustained growth can therefore reflect real data growth, delayed cleanup, insufficient reusable space, workload churn, index growth, TOAST growth, or combinations of these factors.

## 4.11.7 UPDATE and DELETE Lifecycle

Under MVCC, UPDATE commonly creates a new tuple version while the previous version remains until it is no longer visible to any relevant transaction and can be cleaned up. DELETE similarly leaves a row version until it becomes removable.

This explains why a high-churn table can consume substantially more physical storage than its current logical row set might suggest.

Detailed MVCC and dead-tuple behavior follows in Section 4.12.

## 4.11.8 Standard VACUUM

Routine VACUUM is designed primarily to make dead-tuple space reusable and maintain healthy PostgreSQL operation. It is not a general-purpose file-compaction operation.

Operationally:

```text
Dead tuples
     |
     v
  VACUUM
     |
     v
Reusable space inside relation
     |
     v
Future INSERT/UPDATE may reuse it
```

Standard VACUUM can sometimes return trailing empty pages to the operating system when PostgreSQL can safely truncate the relation, but administrators must not assume every VACUUM will reduce the file size.

## 4.11.9 Relation Truncation

Space at the physical end of a table has different reclamation potential from free space in the middle.

```text
Relation file
+------+------+------+------+------+
| used | free | used | free | free |
+------+------+------+------+------+
                  ^            ^
                  |            |
        Internal reuse      Trailing free
                            pages may be
                            truncatable
```

Free pages in the middle normally remain part of the relation and are candidates for reuse. Entirely free trailing pages may be removable from the relation when PostgreSQL can safely perform truncation.

## 4.11.10 `VACUUM FULL`

`VACUUM FULL` uses a fundamentally different strategy from routine VACUUM. It rewrites the table into a new file, allowing stronger compaction and operating-system space reclamation.

It also has important production costs: it requires an `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE` lock and needs additional disk space while the rewritten copy exists.

Therefore:

> `VACUUM FULL` is not routine space maintenance and must not be the default response to normal internal free space.

Detailed operational treatment belongs in Section 4.13.

## 4.11.11 `TRUNCATE` and `DROP`

When the business requirement is to remove an entire table's contents, `TRUNCATE` has very different storage behavior from deleting every row individually.

Similarly, dropping an object removes its relation storage rather than leaving individual dead tuples for normal cleanup.

These operations have locking, transactional, dependency, and application implications and should be chosen because they match the intended data lifecycle, not merely as emergency disk-cleanup shortcuts.

## 4.11.12 Table Rewrites

Some maintenance and DDL operations create a new physical representation of a relation. Rewrites can temporarily require substantial additional capacity because old and new physical structures can coexist during the operation.

A production capacity model must therefore consider:

```text
Current relation size
+
Rewrite working requirement
+
Indexes / TOAST effects
+
WAL generation
+
Safety headroom
```

Never schedule a large rewrite based only on the final expected compacted size.

## 4.11.13 Index Space Lifecycle

Indexes have their own physical storage and cleanup behavior. Table cleanup does not imply that an index immediately becomes physically minimal.

Storage analysis must therefore separate:

```text
Heap
Indexes
TOAST
FSM/VM forks
Temporary storage
WAL
```

Index bloat and remediation are treated in Section 4.14.

## 4.11.14 TOAST Space Lifecycle

Large values may be stored in a table's associated TOAST relation. Capacity analysis that considers only the main heap can therefore miss a material part of the storage lifecycle.

For large-value workloads, inspect heap, indexes, TOAST table, and TOAST indexes together. Section 4.7 contains the detailed TOAST architecture.

## 4.11.15 Long-Running Transactions

A major production risk is assuming that dead-looking data is immediately reusable. Long-running transactions can preserve visibility horizons and delay removal of old tuple versions.

Operationally:

```text
High UPDATE/DELETE churn
        +
Long-lived transaction
        |
        v
Old versions remain necessary longer
        |
        v
Cleanup/reuse delayed
        |
        v
Storage growth risk
```

This interaction will be expanded in Sections 4.12 and 4.13.

## 4.11.16 Logical Size vs. Physical Size

A table containing 100 GB of logically useful data may occupy substantially more than 100 GB physically because of page layout, dead tuples, reusable free space, indexes, TOAST, alignment, and relation forks.

Capacity management must therefore avoid treating application row counts or logical payload estimates as physical storage measurements.

## 4.11.17 Measuring Relation Size

Useful PostgreSQL functions include:

```sql
SELECT pg_size_pretty(pg_relation_size('schema.table_name'));

SELECT pg_size_pretty(pg_table_size('schema.table_name'));

SELECT pg_size_pretty(pg_indexes_size('schema.table_name'));

SELECT pg_size_pretty(pg_total_relation_size('schema.table_name'));
```

These answer different storage questions. Production dashboards should clearly label which definition is being graphed.

## 4.11.18 Size Trend Is More Important Than a Single Size

A single measurement such as:

```text
Table = 800 GB
```

is insufficient for capacity decisions.

More useful context is:

```text
Current size
Growth per day/week
DML churn
Dead tuple trend
Vacuum behavior
Index growth
TOAST growth
Filesystem free space
Capacity runway
```

Trend analysis is developed further in Section 4.15.

## 4.11.19 Steady-State Storage

Healthy high-churn PostgreSQL tables do not necessarily remain at their theoretical minimum physical size. A production objective is often a stable operating envelope in which internal free space is continuously reused.

Conceptually:

```text
Growth from DML
      |
      v
Dead tuples
      |
      v
VACUUM cleanup
      |
      v
Reusable internal space
      |
      v
Future DML reuses space
      |
      +----> Stable physical envelope
```

A table that remains physically larger than its live logical data is not automatically unhealthy.

## 4.11.20 When Physical Growth Is a Warning

Investigate when relation growth is inconsistent with expected workload behavior, especially when accompanied by:

- increasing dead tuple counts;
- autovacuum delays or failures;
- long-running transactions;
- rapidly growing indexes;
- heavy UPDATE/DELETE churn;
- insufficient filesystem runway;
- sustained storage I/O pressure;
- unusual TOAST growth;
- a major workload or schema change.

Do not diagnose bloat from total size alone.

## 4.11.21 Production Capacity Model

A practical storage model is:

```text
Persistent live data
+
Expected internal reusable space
+
Indexes
+
TOAST
+
Normal WAL envelope
+
Temporary workload envelope
+
Maintenance/rewrite headroom
+
Growth until next intervention
+
Safety reserve
```

This is more useful than planning capacity from logical data size alone.

## 4.11.22 Space-Reuse Monitoring Model

Monitor four layers:

```text
APPLICATION
   |
   v
DML churn / data lifecycle
   |
   v
POSTGRESQL
   |
   +-- relation size
   +-- dead/live tuple estimates
   +-- vacuum activity
   +-- index/TOAST size
   |
   v
STORAGE
   |
   +-- used/free capacity
   +-- growth rate
   +-- IOPS/throughput/latency
   |
   v
SRE / DBRE
   |
   +-- capacity runway
   +-- abnormal growth
   +-- maintenance readiness
```

## 4.11.23 Failure Scenario -- DELETE Does Not Free Expected Disk

Scenario:

```text
1 TB table
   |
DELETE 70% of rows
   |
Filesystem remains near 1 TB
```

This is not by itself evidence that DELETE or VACUUM failed. Deleted row versions follow MVCC cleanup rules, and routine VACUUM generally makes space reusable within the relation rather than compacting all free space out of the file.

Correct response:

1. Verify transaction horizons and dead-tuple behavior.
2. Confirm VACUUM/autovacuum health.
3. Determine whether the table will reuse the freed internal space.
4. Determine whether operating-system reclamation is actually required.
5. If physical shrinkage is required, evaluate an appropriate rewrite/reclamation method with locking and free-space requirements understood.

## 4.11.24 Failure Scenario -- Rewrite Runs Out of Disk

A DBA sees a 900 GB relation and 1 TB of free filesystem capacity and starts an operation that rewrites the table.

During execution, the new relation, indexes, WAL, and other workload consume the remaining capacity before the old structures can be released.

Lesson:

> Reclamation operations can temporarily require more free space before they return space.

Always model peak working capacity, not only expected final size.

## 4.11.25 Failure Scenario -- Reusable Space Misdiagnosed as Bloat

A table has substantial internal reusable space after normal churn. It is scheduled for an intrusive rewrite solely because its physical size exceeds live tuple estimates.

If the workload will naturally reuse that space, the rewrite can add locking, I/O, WAL, replica lag, and risk without meaningful long-term capacity benefit.

Space-reuse analysis must precede aggressive compaction.

## 4.11.26 SRE/DBRE Operational Rules

1. Distinguish logical data size from physical relation size.
2. Distinguish dead space from reusable internal space.
3. Distinguish PostgreSQL internal reuse from filesystem reclamation.
4. Do not expect DELETE to immediately reduce filesystem usage.
5. Do not expect routine VACUUM to compact every relation.
6. Use the FSM concept when reasoning about internal page reuse.
7. Treat the VM as visibility metadata, not a free-space map.
8. Monitor heap, indexes, TOAST, WAL, and temporary storage separately.
9. Treat long-running transactions as potential blockers to storage cleanup.
10. Prefer stable reuse over repeated intrusive compaction when the workload will reuse space.
11. Model peak rewrite capacity before reclamation operations.
12. Do not diagnose bloat from table size alone.
13. Trend relation growth rather than relying on isolated measurements.
14. Preserve storage headroom for maintenance and emergency recovery.
15. Never use manual deletion of PostgreSQL relation files as a reclamation technique.
