# 4.25 -- Production Storage Incidents and Case Studies

**Status:** Complete -- Revised Final / Canonical Edition

## Purpose

This section converts Chapter 4 principles into production incident patterns. The objective is not to prescribe one command for every incident, but to teach diagnosis, stabilization, safe remediation, and prevention.

## Case 1 -- Historical Purge Does Not Free Disk

A multi-terabyte table has a large DELETE. Logical row count drops, but filesystem usage remains high.

**Diagnosis:** MVCC and routine VACUUM primarily lead to internal reuse; physical shrinkage is a separate decision.

**Response:** confirm transaction/vacuum health, quantify reusable space and future regrowth, protect runway, and only schedule physical reclamation if durable capacity benefit justifies locking/I/O/WAL risk.

**Lesson:** DELETE completion is not filesystem reclamation.

## Case 2 -- Long Transaction Drives Growth

High-update OLTP tables grow while autovacuum is active. One transaction remains open for days.

**Response:** identify owner and business purpose, assess rollback/retry/locks, safely end the blocker when approved, observe cleanup recovery, then fix application transaction handling.

**Lesson:** vacuum effectiveness depends on cleanup eligibility, not just worker activity.

## Case 3 -- WAL Retention Exhausts Capacity

Data growth is normal, but `pg_wal` expands rapidly because archiving or a replication consumer/slot is unhealthy.

**Response:** identify retention cause, restore consumer/archive health or apply supported slot remediation, expand capacity if needed, and never manually delete WAL.

**Lesson:** WAL is an independent capacity domain.

## Case 4 -- Temp Spill Storm

Concurrent analytical queries spill heavily and fill temporary storage while persistent relation growth is normal.

**Response:** identify spill-heavy plans and concurrency, stop optional offenders, restore capacity, improve plans/statistics/indexes, and use workload-specific memory controls with concurrency analysis.

**Lesson:** capacity incidents can be transient workload incidents.

## Case 5 -- Maintenance Saturates I/O

Backup, VACUUM, and an index build overlap. Disk is not full, but application latency breaches SLO.

**Response:** correlate I/O latency/queueing with maintenance, pause/throttle optional work, reschedule competing operations, and update maintenance performance budgets.

**Lesson:** performance headroom is separate from free-space headroom.

## Case 6 -- Bloat Remediation Creates More Risk

A large relation is labeled bloated from a simple estimate and scheduled for rewrite. The table would have naturally reused most space.

**Response:** validate heap/index/TOAST separately, forecast regrowth, and compare durable savings with rewrite cost.

**Lesson:** reusable space is not automatically waste.

## Case 7 -- Capacity Alert Arrives Too Late

A percentage-only alert fires with hours of runway remaining, but storage expansion needs a day.

**Response:** stabilize workload, expand urgently, then replace threshold-only monitoring with free-byte, growth, runway, and lead-time alerting.

**Lesson:** alerts must preserve response time.

## Case 8 -- Kubernetes PVC Constraint

PostgreSQL growth reaches PVC capacity, but expansion is blocked by StorageClass or platform policy.

**Response:** identify PVC/StorageClass/operator constraints, protect remaining capacity, follow supported migration/expansion procedure, and validate topology/HA.

**Lesson:** database capacity planning must include orchestration-layer limits.

## Case 9 -- Managed-Service Maximum Approaches

Autoscaling masks steady growth until the service approaches its configured/provider maximum.

**Response:** forecast maximum-limit runway, reduce avoidable growth, plan architecture/storage changes early, and use provider-supported modifications.

**Lesson:** autoscaling delays intervention; it does not remove capacity limits.

## RCA Template

For every significant storage incident record timeline, customer/application impact, exact failure domain, trigger, contributing factors, detection quality, forecast accuracy, remediation, risky actions avoided/taken, recovery validation, HA/backup effects, and preventive actions with owners.

## Chapter 4 Canonical Lessons

1. PostgreSQL storage must be understood from pages and tuple lifecycle through infrastructure capacity.
2. MVCC, VACUUM, WAL, temporary work, indexes, and TOAST create distinct storage behaviors.
3. Internal reusable space differs from filesystem free space.
4. Capacity and performance are separate but interacting failure dimensions.
5. Growth trajectory and runway matter more than isolated size.
6. Every independent storage domain requires monitoring and alerts.
7. Safe emergency response stabilizes capacity before aggressive reclamation.
8. Never manually delete PostgreSQL-managed relation or WAL files.
9. Automation should favor observation and forecasting over destructive action.
10. Incidents must improve forecasts, alerts, runbooks, and architecture.
