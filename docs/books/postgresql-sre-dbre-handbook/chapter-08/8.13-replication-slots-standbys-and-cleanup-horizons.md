# 8.13 — Replication Slots, Standbys, and Cleanup Horizons

**Edition:** Revised Final / Canonical  
**Book:** PostgreSQL SRE/DBRE Handbook  
**Chapter 8:** VACUUM, Autovacuum, Freezing, and Transaction-Age Management  
**Technical Baseline:** PostgreSQL 18  
**Status:** **COMPLETE — CANONICAL**

## Purpose

Replication changes what PostgreSQL is allowed to discard. Physical standbys, logical-decoding consumers, replication slots, and standby feedback can establish persistent retention requirements affecting WAL recycling, heap and catalog cleanup, VACUUM effectiveness, storage consumption, standby conflicts, logical-decoding continuity, and failover readiness.

A replication slot is therefore a persistent retention obligation, not merely evidence that a replica exists.

## Core retention horizons

Four fields are central to SRE/DBRE investigation:

- `xmin` — data-cleanup horizon. VACUUM cannot remove tuple versions still protected by the slot horizon.
- `catalog_xmin` — catalog-cleanup horizon, especially important to logical decoding.
- `restart_lsn` — oldest WAL position that might still be required by the consumer.
- `confirmed_flush_lsn` — logical-consumer acknowledgement position; it is not equivalent to `restart_lsn`.

A stalled downstream consumer can therefore create two distinct primary-side problems: WAL retention and restricted tuple/catalog cleanup.

## PostgreSQL 18 slot inventory

```sql
SELECT
    slot_name,
    slot_type,
    database,
    active,
    active_pid,
    xmin,
    catalog_xmin,
    restart_lsn,
    confirmed_flush_lsn,
    wal_status,
    safe_wal_size,
    inactive_since,
    invalidation_reason,
    two_phase,
    two_phase_at,
    failover,
    synced
FROM pg_replication_slots
ORDER BY slot_name;
```

This query is read-only. Activity alone is not health: an active slot can be severely behind, while an inactive slot can represent a legitimate temporarily disconnected consumer.

## WAL retention and capacity

`restart_lsn` represents the WAL-retention horizon. When a consumer stalls, `restart_lsn` can stop advancing while WAL generation continues, causing retained WAL and `pg_wal` pressure to grow.

A useful read-only estimate is:

```sql
SELECT
    slot_name,
    slot_type,
    active,
    restart_lsn,
    pg_size_pretty(
        pg_wal_lsn_diff(pg_current_wal_lsn(), restart_lsn)
    ) AS wal_distance
FROM pg_replication_slots
WHERE restart_lsn IS NOT NULL
ORDER BY pg_wal_lsn_diff(pg_current_wal_lsn(), restart_lsn) DESC;
```

This is LSN distance, not an exact filesystem measurement of `pg_wal` usage.

PostgreSQL 18 provides `max_slot_wal_keep_size` to bound slot-driven WAL retention. The default `-1` permits unlimited retention. A finite value bounds primary exposure but means a sufficiently lagging consumer can lose required WAL.

Capacity planning must therefore consider WAL generation rate, expected consumer outage, storage headroom, recovery requirements, and operational margin.

## `wal_status` and `safe_wal_size`

PostgreSQL 18 exposes `wal_status` states `reserved`, `extended`, `unreserved`, and `lost`.

- `reserved` — required WAL remains retained within normal retention expectations.
- `extended` — required WAL remains available even though retention has extended beyond normal `max_wal_size` behavior.
- `unreserved` — required WAL is no longer guaranteed to remain retained and may be removed during subsequent checkpoint processing.
- `lost` — required WAL has been lost and the slot can no longer continue from its required position.

`safe_wal_size` estimates how many additional WAL bytes can be generated before a slot is in danger of reaching `lost`. Where applicable, `safe_wal_size / WAL generation rate` provides an estimated slot runway.

## Idle slots and invalidation

PostgreSQL 18 supports `idle_replication_slot_timeout`; its default is `0`, which disables automatic idle-slot invalidation. When configured, eligible slots can be invalidated after remaining inactive beyond the configured duration, with enforcement occurring during checkpoint processing.

Important `invalidation_reason` values include `wal_removed`, `rows_removed`, `wal_level_insufficient`, and `idle_timeout`.

Timeout-based invalidation is not a substitute for replication-slot lifecycle governance.

## Logical decoding and two-phase state

Logical slots can retain both WAL and catalog history. Monitor `restart_lsn` together with `catalog_xmin`, and monitor `confirmed_flush_lsn` for consumer acknowledgement.

PostgreSQL 18 also exposes `two_phase` and `two_phase_at` for logical slots configured to decode prepared transactions. Environments using prepared transactions or distributed transaction managers should include these fields in operational inventory.

## Standbys and `hot_standby_feedback`

Standby queries can conflict with cleanup performed on the primary. `hot_standby_feedback` communicates transaction-horizon requirements upstream and can reduce cleanup-related standby cancellations, but it can also delay upstream cleanup and increase bloat risk.

Physical replication slots and standby feedback are related but distinct: a physical slot protects required WAL through `restart_lsn` and provides persistent upstream replication state, while hot-standby feedback communicates `xmin`/`catalog_xmin` requirements upstream.

Do not enable `hot_standby_feedback` merely as a generic fix for standby query cancellation. Evaluate standby-query requirements, primary bloat risk, replication topology, and cleanup horizons together.

## Cascading replication

In cascading replication, a downstream standby depends on an upstream standby rather than directly on the primary. Troubleshooting must therefore map consumer, upstream server, upstream slot, and downstream dependencies before changing replication state.

## Failover logical slots

PostgreSQL 18 supports logical slots configured for failover and exposes `failover` and `synced` state.

`failover = true` indicates configuration intent; it does not prove failover readiness. Before relying on a slot during promotion, verify the intended standby is healthy, the slot is synchronized, required WAL remains available, and the failover procedure has been validated.

`synchronized_standby_slots` can make logical failover-slot progress depend on designated physical standby slots. A stalled, missing, or invalidated required physical slot can therefore become a logical-replication availability incident.

## Slot ownership and destructive operations

Never drop a production slot solely because `active = false`.

Before `pg_drop_replication_slot()` is considered, establish the slot owner, consumer, business purpose, HA/CDC/failover role, reason for inactivity, recovery and rebuild requirements, data-continuity requirements, and approved change plan.

An unknown slot is an investigation problem, not a deletion candidate.

Do not implement unconditional automation such as "inactive for N hours -> drop slot". Safe automation should detect, classify, measure retention, calculate runway, identify ownership, and alert or escalate. Destructive remediation belongs to a governed lifecycle procedure.

## Production diagnostic runbook

1. Inventory all slots and classify physical versus logical.
2. Identify inactive slots and inspect `inactive_since`.
3. Measure WAL distance from `restart_lsn`.
4. Inspect `wal_status`, `safe_wal_size`, and `invalidation_reason`.
5. Inspect `xmin` and `catalog_xmin` for cleanup retention.
6. Identify the actual standby, subscriber, CDC system, migration process, or other consumer.
7. Verify application/platform/HA ownership.
8. Measure `pg_wal` or platform storage usage, free space, WAL generation rate, and storage performance.
9. Estimate storage runway and slot runway where applicable.
10. Correlate old cleanup horizons with dead tuples, relation growth, and VACUUM effectiveness.
11. For failover slots, verify `failover`, `synced`, standby health, and required WAL availability.
12. Select remediation only after the root cause and continuity requirements are understood.
13. Verify recovery through horizon advancement and capacity recovery.
14. Correct ownership, monitoring, capacity, recovery, and decommission governance gaps.

## Recovery criteria

Do not close an incident merely because a consumer reconnects. Verify, where applicable:

- `restart_lsn` advances;
- `confirmed_flush_lsn` advances;
- `safe_wal_size` stabilizes or improves;
- retained WAL stops growing uncontrollably;
- `xmin` and `catalog_xmin` advance;
- VACUUM effectiveness improves;
- storage runway recovers.

## Production principles

1. Replication slots create persistent retention obligations.
2. Physical and logical slots have different operational semantics.
3. `restart_lsn` is a WAL-retention horizon.
4. `xmin` is a data-cleanup horizon.
5. `catalog_xmin` is a catalog-cleanup horizon.
6. `confirmed_flush_lsn` represents logical-consumer acknowledgement.
7. Active does not necessarily mean healthy; inactive does not necessarily mean abandoned.
8. Unlimited slot WAL retention can threaten primary availability.
9. Finite retention trades consumer continuity for bounded primary exposure.
10. Monitor retention velocity and runway, not only absolute retained bytes.
11. `hot_standby_feedback` trades some standby conflicts for upstream retention.
12. VACUUM activity does not prove unrestricted cleanup.
13. Persistent slots require explicit ownership and capacity planning.
14. Slot deletion requires consumer-impact analysis.
15. `failover = true` does not by itself prove failover readiness; synchronization must be verified.
16. Cascading and synchronized replication require topology-aware troubleshooting.
17. Automatic deletion of unknown or inactive slots is unsafe.
18. Incident closure requires measurable horizon and capacity recovery.

## Key takeaway

Replication retention has independent WAL, data, and catalog dimensions. Do not judge replication health merely by whether a slot is active or a standby is connected. Judge it by whether required WAL and cleanup horizons are advancing at a rate that preserves replication continuity, VACUUM effectiveness, and primary storage runway.

A stalled downstream consumer can become an upstream primary-database incident.

The desired production state is: known consumer, known owner, advancing horizons, bounded and understood retention, sufficient capacity, measured runway, verified failover state, and a tested recovery procedure.
