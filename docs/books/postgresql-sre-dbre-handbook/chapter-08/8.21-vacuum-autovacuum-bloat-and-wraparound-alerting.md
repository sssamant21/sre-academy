# 8.21 — Vacuum, Autovacuum, Bloat, and Wraparound Alerting

**Edition:** Revised Final / Canonical  
**Book:** PostgreSQL SRE & DBRE Handbook  
**Chapter 8:** Vacuum, Autovacuum, Bloat, and Maintenance  
**Technical Baseline:** PostgreSQL 18  
**Status:** **COMPLETE — CANONICAL**

## 8.21.1 Purpose

PostgreSQL maintenance alerting should identify developing operational risk early enough for an SRE or DBRE to investigate before the system reaches:

- transaction ID exhaustion;
- MultiXact exhaustion;
- ineffective vacuum cleanup;
- uncontrolled heap or index growth;
- autovacuum starvation;
- storage exhaustion;
- replication retention incidents;
- severe replica impact;
- application-impacting maintenance emergencies.

The goal is not to page merely because `n_dead_tup` is high or VACUUM has been running for a long time.

The goal is to detect sustained maintenance conditions with negative trend, failed or insufficient recovery, shrinking operational runway, and actionable production risk.

> **Alert on sustained maintenance risk, failed recovery, retained correctness horizons, and shrinking runway—not on isolated vacuum metrics without workload context.**

## 8.21.2 Observability Versus Alerting

Section 8.20 established the observability model.

Observability answers:

- What is happening?
- Why is it happening?
- Is maintenance progressing?
- Is cleanup effective?

Alerting answers a different question:

> Does this condition require human attention now?

Not every observable should become an alert. For example, `VACUUM phase = scanning heap` is useful diagnostic context but is not normally actionable by itself.

## 8.21.3 Alert Design Principles

Production maintenance alerts should be:

- actionable;
- sustained rather than transient;
- workload-aware;
- severity-aware;
- trend-aware;
- recovery-aware;
- resistant to flapping;
- deduplicated;
- associated with an owner;
- accompanied by diagnostic context;
- linked to a runbook.

Avoid alert definitions that reduce production health to `metric > threshold` without explaining what the responder should investigate or do.

## 8.21.4 Maintenance Alert Hierarchy

A practical hierarchy is:

```text
Correctness risk
    |
    +-- XID wraparound
    +-- MXID wraparound

Cleanup failure
    |
    +-- cleanup lag
    +-- retained cleanup horizon
    +-- worker starvation

Capacity risk
    |
    +-- heap/index bloat
    +-- storage exhaustion
    +-- WAL retention

Performance risk
    |
    +-- maintenance resource saturation
    +-- replica impact
    +-- application impact

Efficiency risk
    |
    +-- excessive maintenance cost
```

A general operational priority is:

```text
correctness
    >
availability
    >
capacity
    >
performance
    >
efficiency
```

This is an SRE/DBRE prioritization model, not a PostgreSQL-defined severity system.

## 8.21.5 Severity Model

Organizations can map maintenance conditions into their own incident taxonomy.

A simple example is:

```text
INFO
Condition is useful for trend analysis but does not require immediate human intervention.

WARNING
Condition requires investigation while meaningful recovery runway remains.

HIGH
Condition is progressing toward production impact, capacity exhaustion, or correctness risk.

CRITICAL
Immediate or near-immediate risk of transaction-ID failure, storage exhaustion,
severe replication impact, or equivalent production outage.
```

These severity names are organizational policy labels. They are not PostgreSQL-defined severities.

## 8.21.6 Severity Is Not Metric Magnitude

A very large number is not automatically a critical incident.

Severity should consider correctness risk, remaining runway, rate of deterioration, normal recovery capability, business criticality, and current production impact.

For example, a large dead-tuple population that recovers normally after each autovacuum cycle can be less urgent than a smaller backlog that is growing rapidly and cannot be cleaned.

## 8.21.7 Static Thresholds Are Insufficient

Avoid production policies such as:

```text
n_dead_tup > 1,000,000
=> CRITICAL
```

without relation and workload context.

One million dead tuples can be negligible on a multi-billion-row high-churn relation and severe on a much smaller latency-sensitive table.

Use absolute backlog, relative backlog, growth rate, maintenance cadence, recovery after maintenance, and business criticality together.

## 8.21.8 Duration Matters

Transient conditions should generally not page.

A dead-tuple ratio above a threshold for only a brief period may simply reflect normal churn. Prefer conditions that persist for multiple evaluation periods or worsen across multiple maintenance cycles before escalation.

## 8.21.9 Recovery Matters

A high value that recovers naturally may represent healthy maintenance.

Example:

```text
20M dead tuples
      |
      v
autovacuum runs
      |
      v
4M dead tuples
```

A more concerning pattern is:

```text
20M -> VACUUM -> 17M
30M -> VACUUM -> 28M
45M -> VACUUM -> 43M
```

The second pattern shows that maintenance is progressively failing to restore a sustainable baseline.

## 8.21.10 Trend Before Threshold

For maintenance risk, current value should usually be combined with direction, rate of change, and recovery rate.

For example, the same XID age has different operational meaning when it is stable versus increasing rapidly.

# Transaction ID Alerting

## 8.21.11 Database-Level XID Monitoring

Transaction ID wraparound is a correctness risk.

Monitor database-level age with:

```sql
SELECT
    datname,
    age(datfrozenxid) AS xid_age
FROM pg_database
ORDER BY age(datfrozenxid) DESC;
```

This should normally be the primary paging level for XID risk. Relation-level ages should then be attached as diagnostic context.

## 8.21.12 PostgreSQL 18 XID Maintenance Boundaries

PostgreSQL 18 has several distinct XID maintenance boundaries:

```text
normal freeze maintenance
        |
        v
autovacuum_freeze_max_age
        |
        v
forced anti-wraparound VACUUM
        |
        v
vacuum_failsafe_age
        |
        v
failsafe emergency behavior
        |
        v
native wraparound warning
        |
        v
XID allocation shutdown
```

These boundaries must not be treated as one threshold.

## 8.21.13 `autovacuum_freeze_max_age`

PostgreSQL 18 defaults:

```text
autovacuum_freeze_max_age = 200,000,000
```

This controls when PostgreSQL forces anti-wraparound maintenance based on relation age. It is not the end of the XID address space.

Therefore, do not call `age(datfrozenxid) / autovacuum_freeze_max_age` generic XID utilization. Use **anti-wraparound trigger utilization** instead.

## 8.21.14 Anti-Wraparound Trigger Utilization

A useful derived operational metric is:

```text
anti-wraparound trigger utilization
=
current XID age
/
autovacuum_freeze_max_age
```

This describes proximity to the configured forced-maintenance threshold. It does not represent percentage of the entire XID wraparound space consumed.

## 8.21.15 Config-Aware XID Query

```sql
WITH cfg AS (
    SELECT
        current_setting('autovacuum_freeze_max_age')::numeric
            AS freeze_max_age,
        current_setting('vacuum_failsafe_age')::numeric
            AS failsafe_age
)
SELECT
    d.datname,
    age(d.datfrozenxid) AS xid_age,
    round(
        100.0 * age(d.datfrozenxid) / cfg.freeze_max_age,
        2
    ) AS antiwraparound_trigger_pct,
    cfg.freeze_max_age,
    cfg.failsafe_age
FROM pg_database AS d
CROSS JOIN cfg
ORDER BY age(d.datfrozenxid) DESC;
```

Any alert thresholds built on this percentage are organizational policy, not PostgreSQL recommendations.

## 8.21.16 `vacuum_failsafe_age`

PostgreSQL 18 defaults:

```text
vacuum_failsafe_age = 1,600,000,000
```

This is a last-resort correctness mechanism. When the failsafe activates, PostgreSQL prioritizes wraparound avoidance by reducing or bypassing some normal maintenance behavior that could slow completion.

Production alerting should occur long before this boundary.

## 8.21.17 Failsafe Is Not a Normal Alert Threshold

Avoid waiting for `vacuum_failsafe_age` before starting investigation.

The correct operational model is:

```text
warning
        |
        v
high risk
        |
        v
urgent intervention
        |
        v
forced anti-wraparound maintenance
        |
        v
failsafe
```

Failsafe behavior indicates that normal maintenance has already fallen seriously behind.

## 8.21.18 Native XID Emergency Safeguards

If old XIDs are not cleared, PostgreSQL eventually emits wraparound warnings when the database is roughly tens of millions of transactions from the wraparound boundary.

When only a few million transaction IDs remain, PostgreSQL can refuse new transaction ID assignment to protect data correctness.

These are emergency safeguards, not desirable production alert thresholds.

> **Production alerting should provide substantial intervention runway before PostgreSQL reaches its native emergency warnings.**

## 8.21.19 XID Warning Condition

A reasonable organizational warning model is:

```text
XID age approaching organization-defined maintenance-risk range
+
age increasing
+
normal freeze progress insufficient
```

The exact warning threshold should consider transaction generation rate, relation sizes, vacuum throughput, maintenance windows, infrastructure capacity, and business criticality.

## 8.21.20 XID High Condition

Escalate when XID age continues increasing, anti-wraparound activity is ineffective, and remaining runway is shrinking.

Additional escalation evidence includes:

- old blocking transaction;
- old prepared transaction;
- worker saturation;
- storage saturation;
- relation freeze horizon not advancing.

## 8.21.21 XID Critical Condition

A critical XID condition exists when estimated recovery runway is shorter than the organization-defined emergency response window, especially when required anti-wraparound maintenance cannot make sufficient progress.

The severity should reflect both remaining age runway and the observed ability to recover.

## 8.21.22 Relation-Level XID Investigation

Use relation-level age to identify the objects responsible for database risk:

```sql
SELECT
    n.nspname AS schema_name,
    c.relname,
    c.relkind,
    age(c.relfrozenxid) AS xid_age
FROM pg_class AS c
JOIN pg_namespace AS n
  ON n.oid = c.relnamespace
WHERE c.relkind IN ('r', 'm', 't')
ORDER BY age(c.relfrozenxid) DESC
LIMIT 100;
```

Including TOAST relations is useful during correctness investigations.

## 8.21.23 XID Alert Deduplication

Avoid one database XID alert plus hundreds of relation alerts for the same incident.

Prefer one database-level XID incident with top oldest relations, current freeze activity, blockers, and growth rate attached as diagnostic context.

# MultiXact Alerting

## 8.21.24 MXID Monitoring

MultiXact wraparound is a separate correctness dimension.

Monitor:

```sql
SELECT
    datname,
    mxid_age(datminmxid) AS mxid_age
FROM pg_database
ORDER BY mxid_age(datminmxid) DESC;
```

XID health does not guarantee MXID health.

## 8.21.25 PostgreSQL 18 MXID Boundaries

PostgreSQL 18 defaults include:

```text
autovacuum_multixact_freeze_max_age = 400,000,000
vacuum_multixact_failsafe_age = 1,600,000,000
```

These represent different maintenance boundaries.

## 8.21.26 MXID Alerting Model

Use current MXID age, MXID generation rate, freeze progress, configured forced-maintenance boundary, configured failsafe boundary, and remaining operational runway together for alerting.

Avoid using a single fixed `mxid_age()` threshold without configuration context.

## 8.21.27 MXID Member Storage

MultiXact risk can also involve growth of the underlying MultiXact member storage.

Therefore D-MXID investigations should consider:

- `mxid_age(datminmxid)`;
- relation-level `mxid_age(relminmxid)`;
- MultiXact generation rate;
- lock-heavy workload patterns;
- `pg_multixact` growth where infrastructure visibility permits.

MXID health is not purely an age problem.

## 8.21.28 Combined Correctness Query

```sql
SELECT
    datname,
    age(datfrozenxid) AS xid_age,
    mxid_age(datminmxid) AS mxid_age
FROM pg_database
ORDER BY age(datfrozenxid) DESC;
```

Track both values independently.

## 8.21.29 D-XID and D-MXID

Use separate incident classifications:

```text
D-XID
Transaction ID correctness risk

D-MXID
MultiXact correctness risk
```

This immediately points responders toward the correct diagnostic path.

## 8.21.30 Anti-Wraparound Maintenance Despite Normal Autovacuum Disablement

PostgreSQL retains anti-wraparound protection even when ordinary autovacuum behavior is disabled in certain ways.

Therefore, normal autovacuum disabled does not automatically mean anti-wraparound protection disabled.

The operational question is whether required anti-wraparound maintenance is completing successfully.

# Cleanup Backlog Alerting

## 8.21.31 Dead-Tuple Backlog Alerts

Do not alert based only on `n_dead_tup`.

Use absolute dead tuples, dead tuple ratio, growth rate, vacuum activity, and recovery after maintenance together.

Remember that `n_dead_tup` is an estimate.

## 8.21.32 Cleanup Lag Warning

A reasonable warning condition is persistent dead-tuple backlog growth while maintenance is active and recovery is becoming weaker.

This can indicate that maintenance throughput is falling behind workload churn.

## 8.21.33 Cleanup Lag High Alert

Escalate when backlog continues increasing and multiple VACUUM cycles fail to reduce it materially.

This is stronger evidence than any single dead-tuple snapshot.

## 8.21.34 Last Successful Recovery

For high-value relations, consider tracking the last VACUUM cycle that materially reduced backlog.

This can be more useful than only `last_autovacuum` because maintenance recency does not prove maintenance effectiveness.

## 8.21.35 Cleanup Lag Versus Cleanup Blockage

Keep these incident classes separate.

```text
Class B — Cleanup Lag
VACUUM can clean, but throughput is insufficient.

Class C — Cleanup Blocked
VACUUM cannot clean required versions because a horizon is retained.
```

This distinction prevents incorrect remediation.

## 8.21.36 Do Not Tune Around a Blocker

If cleanup is blocked by an old visibility horizon, increasing autovacuum workers, vacuum memory, or vacuum aggressiveness may add work without removing the root cause.

Always eliminate retained-horizon causes before classifying the problem as insufficient maintenance throughput.

# Cleanup-Horizon Alerts

## 8.21.37 Class C Taxonomy

Use:

```text
C1 — Transaction / snapshot horizon
C2 — Replication-slot xmin / catalog_xmin horizon
C3 — Standby-feedback horizon
C4 — Prepared-transaction horizon
```

This gives incident responders a consistent vocabulary.

## 8.21.38 Long Transaction Alerts

A long transaction becomes maintenance-significant when it is unexpectedly old and retains cleanup visibility.

A stronger alert condition is:

```text
unexpected transaction age
+
old backend_xmin
+
cleanup backlog increasing
```

rather than transaction age alone.

## 8.21.39 Long Transaction Context

Include:

- `pid`;
- `usename`;
- `application_name`;
- `client_addr`;
- `state`;
- `xact_start`;
- transaction age;
- `backend_xmin`;
- query text.

The alert should identify likely application ownership.

## 8.21.40 Idle-in-Transaction Alerts

Idle-in-transaction sessions deserve separate monitoring because they can retain transactional state while doing no productive database work.

Alert on abnormal age relative to application expectations. Do not automatically terminate them.

## 8.21.41 Prepared Transaction Alerts

Where two-phase commit is used:

```sql
SELECT
    gid,
    prepared,
    owner,
    database
FROM pg_prepared_xacts
ORDER BY prepared;
```

Alert when prepared transactions become unexpectedly old relative to normal workflow. Do not alert merely because prepared transactions exist.

# Replication Slot Alerting

## 8.21.42 Slot Cleanup Retention Versus WAL Retention

Replication-slot alerts must distinguish:

```text
xmin / catalog_xmin
-> tuple/catalog cleanup retention

restart_lsn
-> WAL retention
```

These represent different operational risks.

## 8.21.43 Slot Observability

```sql
SELECT
    slot_name,
    slot_type,
    active,
    active_pid,
    inactive_since,
    restart_lsn,
    confirmed_flush_lsn,
    xmin,
    catalog_xmin,
    invalidation_reason
FROM pg_replication_slots;
```

## 8.21.44 Slot Cleanup-Horizon Alert

Potential conditions include slot `xmin`/`catalog_xmin` becoming increasingly old, cleanup backlog increasing, and slot consumer progress stopping.

This points toward a C2 cleanup-retention incident.

## 8.21.45 Slot WAL-Retention Alert

Potential conditions include `restart_lsn` significantly behind, retained WAL increasing, and storage runway shrinking.

This is primarily a WAL/storage-capacity incident.

## 8.21.46 Slot Inactivity

Do not define `active = false` as an incident by itself.

An inactive slot may be legitimate. Use inactivity as context together with ownership, expected consumer behavior, cleanup retention, WAL retention, and elapsed inactive time.

## 8.21.47 `inactive_since`

`inactive_since` is useful diagnostic context. It helps responders distinguish a briefly disconnected consumer from a slot that has been inactive for an unexpectedly long period.

It is not, by itself, proof that the slot should be removed.

## 8.21.48 `invalidation_reason`

An invalidated slot is materially different from an ordinary inactive slot.

Possible PostgreSQL-reported reasons can include conditions related to:

- required WAL having been removed;
- required rows being removed;
- insufficient WAL configuration;
- idle timeout.

Treat `invalidation_reason IS NOT NULL` as escalation context.

## 8.21.49 Slot Safety Rule

> **Do not drop an inactive replication slot solely because it is inactive.**

Establish slot, consumer, owner, purpose, retention impact, and recovery consequence before remediation.

# Standby Alerts

## 8.21.50 Standby Conflict Monitoring

On standby servers, monitor `pg_stat_database_conflicts`.

Relevant counters include:

```text
confl_tablespace
confl_lock
confl_snapshot
confl_bufferpin
confl_deadlock
```

These counters are cumulative. Alert on change rate or interval deltas rather than lifetime totals.

## 8.21.51 Conflict Rate Versus Total

Avoid interpreting a high lifetime `confl_snapshot` value as a current incident.

Prefer detecting whether the counter increased rapidly during the current evaluation interval.

## 8.21.52 Standby Conflict and Replication Lag Are Different

A standby can have low replay lag with frequent recovery conflicts, or high replay lag with few cleanup conflicts.

Do not merge these into one alert category.

## 8.21.53 `hot_standby_feedback`

When `hot_standby_feedback` is enabled, standby old snapshots can retain an upstream cleanup horizon.

A meaningful alert condition is a long standby query combined with retained upstream horizon and increasing primary backlog or bloat.

Do not alert merely because `hot_standby_feedback` is enabled.

# Autovacuum Capacity Alerting

## 8.21.54 Effective Worker Capacity

PostgreSQL 18 distinguishes `autovacuum_max_workers` from `autovacuum_worker_slots`.

`autovacuum_max_workers` controls maximum concurrent autovacuum workers.

`autovacuum_worker_slots` defines the worker-process slot pool available for autovacuum workers.

The effective concurrent capacity cannot exceed available worker slots.

## 8.21.55 Worker Configuration Query

```sql
SHOW autovacuum_max_workers;
SHOW autovacuum_worker_slots;
```

Do not assume documentation defaults equal deployed production values.

## 8.21.56 Worker Saturation

A strong worker-saturation signal is:

```text
active workers persistently near effective maximum
+
maintenance backlog increasing
+
critical relations waiting
```

A snapshot where all workers happen to be active does not prove saturation.

## 8.21.57 Cross-Database Starvation

Autovacuum scheduling occurs across databases within the PostgreSQL instance.

A heavily churned database can consume significant worker capacity while maintenance risks rise elsewhere.

Instance alert context should therefore include active workers by database, oldest XID/MXID risk by database, and largest cleanup backlogs.

# VACUUM Progress Alerts

## 8.21.58 Long-Running VACUUM

Do not page simply because VACUUM duration exceeds a fixed time.

Large relations may legitimately require long maintenance.

Interpret duration relative to relation size, historical baseline, current phase, progress rate, waits, and storage performance.

## 8.21.59 Stalled Progress

A stronger condition is:

```text
same VACUUM phase
+
relevant phase counters unchanged
+
condition sustained
```

Then inspect `wait_event`, blockers, storage, and cost delay before assigning root cause.

## 8.21.60 Phase-Aware Progress

Stalled detection must be phase-aware.

During `scanning heap`, monitor `heap_blks_scanned`.

During `vacuuming indexes` or `cleaning up indexes`, use `indexes_total` and `indexes_processed` where applicable.

During `vacuuming heap`, use `heap_blks_vacuumed`.

Do not require heap-scan counters to move during index phases.

## 8.21.61 `delay_time`

PostgreSQL 18 exposes accumulated cost-delay sleep time in VACUUM progress.

This can help distinguish slow progress due to intentional throttling from slow progress due to blocking or resource contention.

## 8.21.62 `track_cost_delay_timing`

`delay_time` requires cost-delay timing collection to be enabled.

PostgreSQL 18 defaults:

```text
track_cost_delay_timing = off
```

Therefore, `delay_time = 0` does not prove that no cost-based throttling occurred. Always check timing configuration.

# Autovacuum Log Alerting

## 8.21.63 `log_autovacuum_min_duration`

PostgreSQL 18 defaults:

```text
log_autovacuum_min_duration = 10min
```

Operationally:

```text
0
-> log every qualifying autovacuum/autoanalyze action

-1
-> disable autovacuum action logging
```

The production value should be selected based on observability requirements and log volume.

## 8.21.64 Slow Autovacuum Logs

Logs can provide evidence of unusually long autovacuum activity, large tuple cleanup, repeated maintenance on one relation, and expensive index processing.

Do not turn every log entry into a page. Use trend and severity context.

## 8.21.65 Skipped Autovacuum

PostgreSQL can log certain autovacuum actions skipped because of conflicting conditions.

One skipped event may be transient.

A stronger alert condition is:

```text
same relation
+
multiple skipped maintenance events
+
backlog or XID/MXID risk increasing
```

# Bloat Alerting

## 8.21.66 Heap Bloat

Do not alert solely from table size.

Large answers how much storage is allocated. It does not answer how much allocation is materially unnecessary.

Bloat requires evidence of persistent excess physical allocation relative to sustainable workload needs.

## 8.21.67 Heap Bloat Alert Inputs

Useful evidence can include physical size growth, stable or declining logical data, persistent excess-space estimate, poor space reuse, and storage or scan cost.

The exact bloat estimation method depends on approved tooling.

## 8.21.68 Bloat Severity Is Impact-Based

Excess allocation plus low storage runway can justify more urgency than excess allocation with large free capacity and no measurable service impact.

Bloat itself does not automatically determine incident severity.

## 8.21.69 Index Bloat

Do not classify a large index as a bloated index.

Review:

- table growth;
- write churn;
- index usage;
- physical inefficiency;
- maintenance history;
- storage impact;
- query performance.

## 8.21.70 REINDEX Is Not an Alert Action

Never design automatic REINDEX directly from an index-bloat alert.

REINDEX requires its own correctness, locking, capacity, WAL, replication, and application-impact gates.

# Storage and WAL Alerting

## 8.21.71 Storage Capacity

Maintenance health depends on available storage.

Monitor percentage used, absolute free bytes, growth rate, WAL retention, and maintenance growth together.

A percentage alone can be misleading.

## 8.21.72 Absolute Capacity Matters

For example, 85% used could leave 20 GB or 20 TB available. Those represent very different operational risk.

## 8.21.73 Storage Runway

A useful derived approximation is:

```text
free bytes
/
recent net growth rate
=
estimated storage runway
```

This is an SRE-derived estimate. Future growth may be nonlinear, so use it together with absolute free space, utilization percentage, workload calendar, and known maintenance activity.

## 8.21.74 WAL Generation Versus WAL Retention

High WAL generation does not necessarily mean an incident.

High generation can be acceptable if archive throughput is sufficient, replicas keep up, and storage remains healthy.

More concerning is `restart_lsn` falling behind while retained WAL increases and storage runway shrinks.

## 8.21.75 Maintenance and WAL

Correlate VACUUM, REINDEX, and rewrite activity with WAL generation, replication state, and storage growth.

Do not infer causation solely from temporal correlation.

# Replica and Application Impact

## 8.21.76 Replica Lag

Maintenance alerts should provide replica lag as context when WAL-heavy operations are occurring.

Do not automatically claim maintenance caused replica lag without supporting evidence.

## 8.21.77 Application Impact

Maintenance severity should increase when it coincides with query latency spikes, transaction latency, timeout increases, retry increases, application errors, or connection-pool pressure.

A maintenance process can be internally effective but operationally unacceptable if it degrades the service.

## 8.21.78 Bloat and Performance Are Separate Evidence Tracks

Do not assume that detected bloat caused current query latency.

Establish bloat evidence and performance evidence separately before claiming causality.

# Alert Correlation and Noise Control

## 8.21.79 Parent-Child Alerts

Use root-cause relationships where possible.

Example:

```text
Storage saturation
    |
    +-- VACUUM slowdown
    +-- replica lag
    +-- query latency
```

The storage event may represent the primary incident. Avoid paging separately on every downstream symptom.

## 8.21.80 Alert Deduplication

Suppose one relation shows dead tuples growing, VACUUM slow, and storage latency high.

Prefer one enriched incident identifying maintenance degradation and likely storage contribution rather than several independent pages.

## 8.21.81 Deduplication Is a Production Requirement

Maintenance incidents frequently surface through multiple correlated metrics.

Without deduplication, responders can receive worker, dead-tuple, storage, replica, and latency alerts for one event.

That makes incident response slower rather than safer.

## 8.21.82 Flapping Protection

Use sustained-duration evaluation, hysteresis, recovery thresholds, multiple evaluation periods, and stateful alerting.

Conceptually:

```text
trigger > X
recover < Y
```

where `Y < X` can reduce repeated open/close cycles.

Any numeric example is alert-engine design guidance, not PostgreSQL threshold guidance.

## 8.21.83 Do Not Reuse Hysteresis Percentages as XID Guidance

An arbitrary hysteresis example must never be presented next to XID or MXID limits in a way that could be mistaken for PostgreSQL wraparound guidance.

PostgreSQL does not define generic 70/60 or similar alert thresholds.

## 8.21.84 Planned Maintenance Suppression

Planned maintenance can intentionally cause high I/O, WAL spikes, replica lag, long maintenance duration, and temporary storage growth.

Maintenance-aware suppression can reduce noise.

However:

> **Do not suppress correctness risks such as XID/MXID exhaustion unless equivalent alternate monitoring remains active.**

# Observability Gap Alerts

## 8.21.85 Missing Critical Telemetry

Missing maintenance telemetry can itself be an alert.

Examples:

```text
XID-age metrics missing
MXID-age metrics missing
replication-slot metrics missing
VACUUM progress collector unavailable
```

If missing telemetry prevents safe risk assessment, classify it as **Class F — Observability Gap**.

## 8.21.86 A Monitoring Failure Can Become a Maintenance Risk

Losing a dashboard is not necessarily production-impacting.

Losing wraparound-runway monitoring for a prolonged period can be.

Alert based on the operational safety value of the missing metric.

# Alert Context

## 8.21.87 Minimum Alert Context

A paging alert should ideally include:

- instance or cluster;
- database;
- schema or relation where relevant;
- classification;
- severity;
- current value;
- configured boundary;
- trend;
- duration;
- estimated runway;
- current VACUUM phase;
- current maintenance duration;
- blocking condition;
- worker utilization;
- storage health;
- WAL state;
- replica state;
- application owner;
- runbook.

## 8.21.88 Include Alert Age

Responders need to know whether the condition has existed for minutes or hours.

Every enriched alert should include first observed, last observed, and condition duration where the monitoring platform supports them.

## 8.21.90 MXID Alert Example

```text

...
```

# Alert Classification

## 8.21.95 Maintenance Classification Map

Use the same classification vocabulary established in Section 8.20:

```text
A — Healthy Maintenance
B — Cleanup Lag
C1 — Transaction / Snapshot Horizon
C2 — Slot xmin / catalog_xmin Horizon
C3 — Standby Feedback Horizon
C4 — Prepared Transaction Horizon
D-XID — Transaction ID Risk
D-MXID — MultiXact Risk
E — Maintenance Resource Saturation
F — Observability Gap
```

This gives observability, alerting, incident response, and runbooks a shared language.

## 8.21.96 Class A

Characteristics:

```text
backlog bounded
maintenance recovery healthy
XID/MXID runway safe
resource impact acceptable
```

Action: no paging; continue monitoring.

## 8.21.97 Class B

Characteristics:

```text
VACUUM able to clean
+
maintenance throughput insufficient
+
backlog increasing
```

Action: investigate workload churn, autovacuum capacity, per-table configuration, maintenance memory, cost delay, and infrastructure.

## 8.21.98 Class C

Characteristics:

```text
VACUUM runs
+
cleanup remains ineffective
+
retained horizon identified
```

Action depends on subtype:

```text
C1 -> transaction/snapshot owner
C2 -> replication-slot consumer
C3 -> standby workload / feedback policy
C4 -> prepared-transaction workflow
```

## 8.21.99 Class D

Correctness risk:

```text
D-XID
or
D-MXID
```

These should generally receive higher urgency than ordinary bloat or maintenance-efficiency problems.

## 8.21.100 Class E

Characteristics:

```text
maintenance progressing
+
CPU / I/O / WAL / replication / application cost unacceptable
```

Action: identify the actual resource bottleneck before changing autovacuum configuration.

## 8.21.101 Class F

Characteristics:

```text
insufficient telemetry
+
maintenance safety cannot be evaluated reliably
```

Action: restore required observability before making aggressive tuning decisions.

# Runbooks and Automation

## 8.21.103 Alert Runbook Contract

Every paging-level alert should answer:

1. What does the alert mean?
2. Why does it matter?
3. What should the responder inspect first?
4. Which root causes are most likely?
5. Which actions are safe?
6. Which actions are dangerous?
7. How is recovery validated?
8. When should the incident escalate?

## 8.21.104 Unsafe Automatic Remediation

Do not automatically:

- terminate transactions;
- drop replication slots;
- run `VACUUM FULL`;
- run `REINDEX`;
- disable autovacuum;
- change freeze limits;
- increase autovacuum workers;
- change vacuum cost settings;

solely because an alert fired.

These actions require diagnosis and explicit production safety gates.

## 8.21.105 Never Change Correctness Limits to Silence Alerts

Do not respond to XID/MXID risk by automatically increasing `autovacuum_freeze_max_age` or `autovacuum_multixact_freeze_max_age` simply to make the alert disappear.

That changes maintenance behavior without resolving the underlying problem.

## 8.21.106 Safe Automation

Good automation candidates include:

- capture `pg_stat_progress_vacuum`;
- capture current VACUUM phase;
- capture oldest XID-age relations;
- capture oldest MXID-age relations;
- capture `pg_stat_activity`;
- capture blocking PIDs;
- capture prepared transactions;
- capture replication-slot state;
- capture PostgreSQL I/O metrics;
- capture WAL statistics;
- capture storage telemetry;
- attach owner metadata;
- attach runbook links.

> **Automate evidence collection aggressively and remediation conservatively.**

## 8.21.107 Dashboard-to-Alert Flow

# Alert Quality Management

## 8.21.108 Alert Validation

Review alerts using:

- true positives;
- false positives;
- false negatives;
- time to acknowledge;
- time to diagnosis;
- time to remediation.

An alert that fires frequently but rarely requires action should be redesigned.

## 8.21.109 Noisy Alerts Must Be Fixed

Do not normalize “this alert always fires” as acceptable production behavior.

Persistent alert noise teaches operators to ignore real incidents.

Tune threshold, duration, rate calculation, hysteresis, deduplication, and severity.

## 8.21.110 Threshold Recalibration

Review maintenance-alert thresholds after:

- significant data growth;
- workload changes;
- schema changes;
- instance resizing;
- storage-tier changes;
- PostgreSQL upgrades;
- autovacuum configuration changes;
- major application releases.

Alert configuration is an ongoing operational process.

## 8.21.111 PostgreSQL 18 I/O Considerations

PostgreSQL 18 includes broader asynchronous I/O capabilities that can alter maintenance performance characteristics.

These are diagnostic context for Class E resource-saturation investigations. They should not become an independent correctness alert.

Interpret I/O behavior according to the deployed PostgreSQL 18 configuration rather than assuming older-version performance characteristics.

# Production Principles

## 8.21.112 Production Alerting Principles

1. **Alert on risk, not merely metric presence.**
2. **Separate observability from paging.**
3. **Correctness risks generally outrank maintenance efficiency concerns.**
4. **Use severity based on risk, runway, deterioration rate, and impact.**
5. **XID and MXID require separate alerting.**
6. **Use database-level XID/MXID alerts and relation-level diagnostic context.**
7. **Do not call the forced-maintenance ratio generic XID utilization.**
8. **Use the term anti-wraparound trigger utilization.**
9. **Distinguish forced anti-wraparound maintenance from failsafe behavior.**
10. **Treat failsafe territory as an emergency condition, not a normal alert target.**
11. **Do not wait for PostgreSQL's native wraparound emergency warnings.**
12. **Use configuration-aware alerting.**
13. **Use growth rate and operational runway where possible.**
14. **Runway is a derived SRE/DBRE metric.**
15. **Dead tuples are estimates.**
16. **A high dead-tuple count is not automatically an incident.**
17. **Alert on persistent backlog and failed recovery.**
18. **Track last successful backlog recovery where practical.**
19. **Distinguish cleanup lag from cleanup blockage.**
20. **Do not tune around retained visibility horizons.**
21. **Long-transaction alerts should include cleanup impact evidence.**
22. **Idle-in-transaction sessions deserve separate attention.**
23. **Prepared transactions require workflow-aware thresholds.**
24. **Separate slot cleanup retention from WAL retention.**
25. **Inactive slots are not automatically abandoned slots.**
26. **Use `invalidation_reason` as escalation context.**
27. **Do not drop replication slots automatically.**
28. **Alert on standby conflict rates, not cumulative counts alone.**
29. **Standby conflicts and replication lag are different signals.**
30. **Treat `hot_standby_feedback` as a cleanup-versus-query tradeoff.**
31. **Worker utilization alone is not saturation.**
32. **Persistent near-capacity workers plus backlog is actionable.**
33. **Account for cross-database maintenance competition.**
34. **Long VACUUM duration alone is not failure.**
35. **Stalled-progress detection must be phase-aware.**
36. **Use phase-relevant progress counters.**
37. **Interpret `delay_time` only with timing configuration awareness.**
38. **Remember `track_cost_delay_timing` is off by default in PostgreSQL 18.**
39. **Use autovacuum logs as historical evidence.**
40. **Repeated skipped maintenance is more significant than one skip.**
41. **Large is not synonymous with bloated.**
42. **Bloat severity should include operational impact.**
43. **Never automatically REINDEX from a bloat alert.**
44. **Monitor both storage percentage and absolute free bytes.**
45. **Use storage runway as an estimate, not a guarantee.**
46. **Separate WAL generation from WAL retention.**
47. **Correlate maintenance with replica and application impact.**
48. **Do not claim maintenance caused service degradation without evidence.**
49. **Use parent-child alert correlation.**
50. **Deduplicate maintenance incidents.**
51. **Use sustained-duration checks and hysteresis.**
52. **Do not present illustrative percentages as PostgreSQL recommendations.**
53. **Do not suppress correctness monitoring during planned maintenance without alternate protection.**
54. **Missing critical telemetry can itself be a production alert.**
55. **Every paging alert should identify an owner and runbook.**
56. **Include alert age and trend where possible.**
57. **Automate evidence collection before remediation.**
58. **Keep destructive actions behind explicit safety gates.**
59. **Never raise freeze limits merely to silence an alert.**
60. **Review false positives and false negatives regularly.**
61. **Recalibrate alerts as workloads and infrastructure change.**
62. **No alert is required when maintenance is healthy and runway is safe.**
63. **The goal is early actionable detection, not maximum alert volume.**

## 8.21.114 Key Takeaway

Vacuum alerting should not ask only whether a metric is high.

It should ask:

```text
Is maintenance risk increasing?
        |
        v
Is normal recovery failing?
        |
        v
Is a cleanup horizon retained?
        |
        v
Is XID or MXID correctness at risk?
        |
        v
Is operational runway shrinking?
        |
        v
Is capacity or service health deteriorating?
        |
        v
Does a human need to act now?
```

> **Alert on sustained maintenance risk, failed recovery, retained correctness horizons, and shrinking runway—not on isolated vacuum metrics without workload context.**

The objective is early evidence, actionable alerting, correct classification, safe investigation, controlled remediation, and validated recovery—not paging everyone whenever a metric changes.
